The quiet work of noticing

At six in the morning, the river looks almost still. Only the leaves gathered behind the bridge reveal its movement. I came here to finish a walk, but stayed to watch a small heron negotiate the shallows. It was a useful reminder: attention is something we practice, not something we simply have.

This stretch of water runs through the middle of the city. Buses cross above it, bakeries open beside it, and most days I pass without looking down. Today I brought a notebook instead of headphones. The experiment was deliberately modest: record three things I would otherwise miss.

Small observations, lasting questions

Naturalists have kept records like these for centuries. The Biodiversity Heritage Library holds thousands of their field journals, full of careful drawings and uncertain conclusions. Their value lies as much in the questions as in the answers.

What went into the notebook

  • A line of ants following a crack in the stone wall.
  • Two different bird calls from the same willow tree.
  • The smell of warm bread arriving before the bakery lights.
    • Time: just after sunrise.
    • Weather: cool, with no wind at the bridge.

“The first step is not to name everything. It is to stay long enough to realize how much you have not seen.”

A list is not the whole experience, of course. The heron never entered my notes until it had flown away. But writing slows the habit of replacing a place with an idea of that place. Look first; explain later.

A repeatable morning experiment

  1. Choose a route you know well and leave ten minutes early.
  2. Stop somewhere safe, away from the flow of commuters.
  3. Write down a sound, a movement, and a change in light.
  4. Return next week and compare what you noticed.

Keeping a small digital record

A plain text file works as well as a notebook. If you prefer something structured, use a short record with a location and an array of observations. Keep the format simple enough that maintaining it does not become the point of the walk. 

const morning = {
    location: 'Willow bridge',
    observations: [
        'Heron fishing in the shallows',
        'Fresh bread from the corner bakery',
    ],
}

for (const note of morning.observations) {
    console.log(`${morning.location}: ${note}`)
}

Leave room for uncertainty

Was it the same bird as last week? Did the water really rise, or was the morning light playing tricks? A useful journal welcomes “I do not know yet.” You can return to the earlier observations without having to turn them into a conclusion.

By the time I headed home, the bridge was busy. Nothing remarkable had happened, and the city had not become quieter. I had simply spent a little longer inside the morning. For today, that was enough.
